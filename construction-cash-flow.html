Why profitable construction jobs run out of cash

Retainage, net-30 and the draw schedule, modelled month by month.

Profit and cash are not the same thing. You pay labor weekly and material on delivery, then wait 30 to 60 days after billing to be paid, minus retainage held until completion. A job earning a healthy 19.0% margin can still fall to a $3,800 cash balance in month four.

The gap, in order

Labor is paid every week it is worked. Material is paid on delivery or on 30-day supplier terms. Subcontractors are paid on completion of their scope. Against that, you bill at a milestone, the client pays you on their terms, and a slice of every payment is withheld as retainage until the project closes out.

Every one of those is normal. Together they mean the money goes out months before it comes back, and no line in your profit calculation shows it.

A worked job

A $148,000 kitchen and bath remodel over six months. Total cost $119,900, including overhead. Profit $28,100, a 19.0% margin. Retainage 10%, payment terms net-30, $25,000 of working capital available.

Lowest cash point: $3,800, in month four. A job with a healthy margin consumed roughly 85% of the available working capital before it ever paid out. Month seven and eight — where the balance finally looks comfortable — are the retainage release and the final draw, long after the work was finished.

Four levers, in order of cost to you

1. Front-load the draw schedule

Shift a larger share of the contract into the early milestones. It costs nothing, it is normal practice, and it moves the low point more than anything else on this list. It is also the only one you control entirely at the negotiating table.

2. Shorten the payment terms

Moving from net-60 to net-30 pulls an entire month of receipts forward. Moving the other way can be enough on its own to turn a comfortable job into a financed one.

3. Negotiate the retainage

Ten percent withheld on a $148,000 contract is $14,800 you cannot spend. Five percent halves that.

4. Arrange credit before you need it

A line of credit opened while the job is healthy costs very little. The same line applied for in month four, with the balance already thin, is a different conversation.

The test to run before signing

Lay the draw schedule against the cost plan month by month and read the lowest balance. If that figure is below the cash you can genuinely put into the job, you have three choices: renegotiate the schedule, arrange credit, or decline. Finding out in month four is not one of them.

Frequently asked questions

Why do profitable construction jobs run out of cash?

Because costs leave before revenue arrives. Labor is paid weekly and material on delivery, while billing is paid 30 to 60 days later, minus retainage held until completion. The profit is real; it just arrives after the spending does.

What is retainage?

A percentage of each progress payment, commonly 5% to 10%, withheld by the client until the project is complete and sometimes for a period afterwards. On a $148,000 contract at 10%, $14,800 is unavailable to you for the length of the job.

How do I fix a cash flow problem on a job?

Front-load the draw schedule so more of the contract is billed early, negotiate shorter payment terms, reduce the retainage percentage, or arrange a line of credit before you need it. The first is free and usually the most effective.

What is a draw schedule?

The agreed list of billing milestones on a project, each carrying a percentage of the contract value. It determines when you can invoice, which determines when you get paid.

How much working capital does a job need?

Model it rather than guess. In the worked example a $148,000 job earning a 19.0% margin still drops to a $3,800 cash balance in month four, meaning it consumed roughly 85% of $25,000 of available working capital at its worst point.
