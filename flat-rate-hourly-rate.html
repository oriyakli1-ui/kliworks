What your flat rate hourly rate should be

Burdened wage, overhead recovery and target margin, built into one number.

Your billable hour rate is the burdened wage plus overhead per hour, divided by one minus your target margin. For a $32 tech at 34% burden carrying $36.80 of overhead at a 20% target margin, the rate is $99.60 per hour — 3.11 times the wage.

The build-up

Tech wage  $32.00
+ labor burden at 34%  →  burdened wage $42.88
+ overhead per billable hour  $36.80
= break-even per hour  $79.68
÷ (1 − 0.20 target margin) = $99.60 billable rate

Each step is a real cost that somebody pays. Skip the burden and you are paying payroll tax out of profit. Skip the overhead and you are paying for your own truck out of profit. Skip the margin and you are working for wages.

The multiple nobody expects

$99.60 against a $32 wage is 3.11 times. The rule of thumb most owners carry is somewhere near 1.5 times, which in this example is $48 an hour — roughly $32 below break-even, before a single dollar of profit.

What undercharging costs

The gap does not feel like much per hour, which is exactly why it survives. At $95 an hour against a true rate of $99.60 the shortfall is $4.60. Across 3,750 billable hours it is $17,250 a year — roughly the profit of a whole additional technician, given away four dollars at a time.

Turning the rate into a price book

A flat rate price is this number multiplied by the labor hours for the task, plus material at your markup, rounded up to a sensible increment and floored at your minimum service call. Build it once and every quote afterwards is a lookup rather than a negotiation.

Add Good / Better / Best tiers on top and the average ticket rises without the base price moving, because the customer is choosing scope rather than deciding whether to buy.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my billable hourly rate?

Add your burdened wage to your overhead recovery per hour to get break-even, then divide by one minus your target margin. A $32 wage at 34% burden is $42.88; add $36.80 of overhead for $79.68 break-even; divide by 0.80 for a $99.60 billable rate.

What is labor burden?

Payroll taxes, workers compensation, liability insurance, paid time off, training and any benefits. It typically adds 28% to 40% on top of the base wage.

How much more than the wage should I charge?

In the worked example the billable rate is 3.11 times the tech wage. Many owners assume 1.5 times, which is roughly break-even before overhead is recovered at all.

Does this apply to flat rate pricing?

Yes, and it is the foundation of it. A flat rate price is labor hours multiplied by this rate, plus material with its markup. If the rate is wrong, every price in your book is wrong by the same proportion.

What happens if I undercharge by a few dollars?

It scales with every billable hour. Billing $95 when the true rate is $99.60 is $4.60 an hour; across 3,750 billable hours that is $17,250 of profit gone in a year.
